How to qualify for a mortgage: A guide to home loan approval
Quick insights
- Mortgage lenders usually review your credit, income, debt obligations, job history and assets when determining whether you might qualify for a home loan.
- Gathering documents like pay stubs, W-2s, tax returns and bank statements in advance may help streamline your mortgage prequalification or mortgage preapproval process. Chase does not offer mortgage prequalification.
- Explore various mortgage options like FHA loans and VA loans to find flexible options for down payments and closing costs.
Purchasing your dream home is a major step, and understanding how to qualify for a mortgage can help you make it a reality. Whether you’ve been saving for years or are just starting to explore your loan options, the process might seem complex at first. This guide will break down mortgage eligibility requirements to help you navigate the home loan approval process with confidence.
Key mortgage qualification factors: What lenders consider
When applying for a home loan, mortgage providers review various factors to help determine whether you might qualify. These factors may influence the loan terms you’re offered, too.
Here are several important factors:
- Credit score: Your credit history plays a huge role in the mortgage approval process. A higher score may help you qualify for better mortgage rates, while a lower score might limit your options. Checking your credit early gives you time to make improvements before applying for a mortgage.
- Debt-to-income ratio (DTI): This measures how much of your income goes toward debt each month. Mortgage lenders prefer a lower DTI ratio because it can signal a manageable debt load.
- Employment and income stability: This includes W-2 income, self-employment income (often requiring two years of tax returns), bonuses, commissions and rental income. Consistent work history and reliable income can help demonstrate financial stability. Lenders typically like to see at least two years of work in the same industry, though this can vary.
- Down payment and savings: While some loan options allow low or no down payment, having a substantial down payment can avoid private mortgage insurance (PMI), reduce monthly payments and help lower your interest rate. Having additional savings shows the loan provider you’re financially prepared for homeownership and related costs, such as closing costs and home maintenance.
- Property type and condition: The type and condition of the property you want to purchase can also influence mortgage qualification. Lenders require appraisals to ensure the home's value supports the loan amount, and certain loan types (like FHA or VA) have specific property standards.
Prepare your finances for mortgage qualification
The earlier you start preparing your finances, the smoother the mortgage process might feel. A few simple steps could help strengthen your application:
- Check your credit report: Obtain your credit report and review it for errors or outdated information that could impact your score. Paying your bills on time and reducing credit card balances can also help improve your financial profile.
- Pay down existing debt: Lowering your debt may reduce your DTI ratio and increase your chances of qualifying for a home loan. In general, credit utilization below 30% may help with mortgage qualification.
- Save for upfront costs: Remember that your down payment isn’t the only upfront expense when buying a home. You’ll also need to budget for closing costs, which usually include appraisal fees, title insurance, home inspection and other charges.
- Avoid major financial changes: During the mortgage prequalification and mortgage preapproval stages, it might be beneficial to hold off on large purchases or new loans that could affect your credit and savings.
Gather key documents for approval
Getting preapproved for a mortgage can help you understand important qualification criteria and a price range to shop in. For preapproval, lenders usually ask for documents that verify your income, assets and debts:
- Recent pay stubs (30-60 days)
- W-2 forms (past two years)
- Federal tax returns (past two years, especially for self-employed individuals)
- Proof of other income (bonuses, commissions, rental income, alimony, child support)
- Bank statements (past 60 days for checking/savings)
- Credit report (the lender will pull this, but review yours beforehand)
- Statements for existing debts (credit cards, student loans, auto loans)
- Driver’s license or state ID
- Social Security card
Having these documents organized and readily available can make your mortgage application process smoother. Responding to requests for additional documents can also ease the process.
Compare different loan options and requirements
Not all mortgages are the same, and the right one for you will depend on your financial situation, location and eligibility. Here are a few different mortgage options you may want to consider.
Conventional loans
Conventional loans are not insured or guaranteed by the government.
Typical requirements:
- Credit score: Generally 620-640+ (higher scores yield better rates).
- Down payment: As low as 3%, but 20% avoids private mortgage insurance (PMI).
- DTI ratio: Typically 36-43%.
- Pros: Flexible terms, can avoid PMI with 20% down, good for strong credit.
- Cons: Stricter credit requirements, PMI if less than 20% down.
FHA loans
Insured by the Federal Housing Administration, FHA loans are designed to help low-to-moderate-income borrowers.
Typical requirements:
- Credit score: As low as 580 for a 3.5% down payment; 500-579 may require 10% down.
- Down payment: Minimum 3.5%.
- DTI ratio: Can be more flexible, up to 43-50% in some cases.
- Pros: Lower credit score requirements, low down payment, assumable.
- Cons: Requires upfront mortgage insurance premium (UFMIP) and annual mortgage insurance premium (MIP) for the life of the loan (unless 10% down and 11 years).
VA loans
A VA loan is guaranteed by the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs for eligible service members, veterans, and surviving spouses.
Typical requirements:
- Credit score: No minimum set by VA, but lenders typically look for 620+.
- Down payment: Often 0% down payment required.
- DTI ratio: Depends on the lender—often up to 41% or higher with residual income.
- Pros: No down payment, no PMI, competitive interest rates, limited closing costs.
- Cons: Requires VA funding fee (can be waived for some), property must meet VA standards.
USDA loans
Backed by the U.S. Department of Agriculture for low-to-moderate-income borrowers in eligible rural areas.
Typical requirements:
- Credit score: No minimum set by USDA, but lenders typically look for 640+.
- Down payment: Often 0% down payment required.
- DTI ratio: Typically 29/41% (housing/total debt).
- Pros: No down payment, low mortgage insurance, competitive rates.
- Cons: Income limits, property location restrictions, requires upfront and annual guarantee fees.
Chase does not offer USDA loans at this time.
In summary
Qualifying for a mortgage may feel like a big task, and it’s key to buying a home. However, preparation and understanding what loan providers look for are helpful. By focusing on your credit, managing debt responsibly, saving for a down payment and exploring different loan programs, you could set yourself up for success. Taking these steps and potentially working with a Home Lending Advisor can help you feel more confident while buying your dream home.
